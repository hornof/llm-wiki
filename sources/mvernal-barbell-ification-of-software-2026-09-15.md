## Summary

**Mike Vernal** (@mvernal) argues that as software-engineering cost approaches zero, the software market re-organizes the way newspapers did when distribution cost went to zero: **a barbell, with the middle gone**. Either you are one of a few very large AI-native systems, or you are a solo/SMB operator — and *"there is no safety in the middle."*

This is the **third distinct answer** the wiki now holds to "what survives when software gets cheap," and it disagrees with the other two. See [[domain-specific-harness]].

## Key Claims / Takeaways

### The analogy

Ben Thompson's newspaper case: regional monopolies rooted in **distribution infrastructure** collapsed when distribution went free. The end state is a barbell — the NYT at **+1,104%** subscribers since 2002 against the LA Times at **−75%**, Chicago Tribune **−76%**, Dallas Morning News **−88%**, Newsday **−91%** — with Lenny's Newsletter at >1.2M subscribers occupying the other end. *(Vernal notes the current-subscriber column was compiled by Claude; treat the individual figures as indicative.)*

### Why software moats erode

The three classic pillars all weaken at once:

- **Cost/complexity of replication** — software becomes faster and cheaper to replicate
- **Switching costs** — migrations can be increasingly automated
- **Network effects** (ecosystem, integrators, human experts) — *"integrations can be automated; the AI is a better expert than your p95 human"*

### What replaces them — scale, not defensibility

His Amazon reading is the load-bearing part: Amazon had *"the least-defensible initial premise of the major tech companies"* — reselling books — and its moat is **7,500+ days of relentless reinvestment**, not any single asset. Applied forward:

> *"If the amount of software you can build in a given day increases by 1,000x, and you build that amount of software every day for 10 years, you probably still have a moat."*

In Helmer's 7 Powers terms: **switching costs and network effects become less important; scale economies and branding become more important.** The dominant strategy becomes *"create an unimaginable amount of software every single day and re-invest the profits."*

### The predicted end state

- **One AI-native system per enterprise buying center** (Sales, Marketing, Finance, HR, IT).
- **One all-in-one system** for SMB/mid-market (he names Rippling).
- **One large software company per industry** (Legal, Finance, Medicine) — *"similar to how there's often one dominant trade magazine by industry."*
- **Most mid-sized point solutions consolidated or dead.**
- **An explosion of "small" software** — mostly people building for themselves, plus niche software businesses echoing the 2010s D2C wave (Shopify + Meta Ads). He expects a power law over "100M apps" built via tools like Lovable.
- For venture-backed companies: *"the answer is once again 'do it all.' Just build the whole thing — your moat is the totality of what you've built and completely owning the buying center."*
- **A caveat he flags himself**: the small-software winners *"will not be 'venture-addressable'… many founders and investors will be led astray by thinking they are."*
