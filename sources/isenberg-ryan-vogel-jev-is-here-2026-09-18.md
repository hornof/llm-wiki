## Summary

[[greg-isenberg|Greg Isenberg]] with **Ryan Vogel**, 28 minutes, published **2026-09-18** — three days after [[jev|Jev]] launched. A practitioner walkthrough of what the model is for and where it pays.

**Listing depth only. No transcript obtained** — this is folded from the published episode description, so quotes below are the description's wording, not verified audio.

## Key Claims / Takeaways

- **The mechanic, restated:** Jev processes inputs against **output schemas** and returns **probability scores**, within **200 milliseconds**.
- **A cost receipt, not a benchmark:** a demo sorting **1,700 emails for 18 cents**. That is ~**$0.0001 per decision** — the first concrete unit cost the wiki holds for Jev, against a stack of vendor percentage claims.
- **The stated scope rule:** *"at any point where a business makes fast, repeatable decisions on incoming data."*
- **Named applications:** lead scoring, support routing, video clipping, browser control.
- **Distribution:** access runs through **Vercel's AI Gateway**, with a waitlist.

## Judgment

**The 18-cents figure is the useful part** and it is consistent with the CMU finding of **0.36% of a strong judge's fee** ([[jev-as-a-judge-arxiv-2609-26550]]) — two independent surfaces pointing at the same order of magnitude, one academic and one demo-grade.

**The Vercel Gateway detail is a distribution fact worth noting**: [[guillermo-rauch|Rauch's]] platform is the access path for the model, which puts Vercel in the position of gatekeeping the cheap-judgment layer. The wiki has no `vercel` page; this is a second surface toward one.

**"Browser control" is listed as an application without explanation.** A 200 ms schema-bound classifier choosing browser actions is a meaningfully different claim from routing support tickets, and the description does not support it.
