## Summary

A ~10–15 minute guide from [[greg-isenberg|Greg Isenberg]] arguing you should **buy an existing services business and use agents to 3× its EBITDA**, rather than build a software product for that industry. It is the companion piece to his [[gregisenberg-ai-native-services-100b-guide-2026-09-20|AI-native services guide]] six days earlier and **inverts its premise**: that one says build the service firm, this one says acquire one.

**The strongest operational document the wiki holds on this thesis**, and the one with the most named receipts. Also the most conflicted — he links his own idea service, his design agency and his podcast.

## The thesis

> *"A startup has to earn the customer and then do the work. An acquisition already has the customer, so all you have to do is change the work."*

**The margin claim:** a traditional services firm runs **5–10% EBITDA**; the thesis is the same firm can run at **30–40%**. Same clients, same invoices, 3–4× the profit. You buy at a services multiple because *"the market assumes their margins are stuck"* — so if you can prove they aren't, you bought at a discount to what the asset is now worth.

**Why now, in his framing:** (1) *"Two years ago the output needed redoing. Now it needs checking."* (2) Owners are retiring with no succession plan — **~$5T of American businesses changing hands by 2035** and **1M+ boomer-owned businesses selling**, per McKinsey. (3) The arithmetic works.

**Why buy rather than build:** the hard parts aren't software. The client list took 30 years, the trust is personal, the licenses took exams, and *"the knowledge of what 'correct' means in the niche lives in the heads of people who've done the work for decades."* Plus the history — every past engagement and caught mistake — *"that's the training material for your agents, and a startup has none of it."*

## Named receipts

| Operator | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Larson Gross** (accounting, Bellingham WA, founded 1949, 5 offices, ~200 staff; Thrive Holdings took a stake) | AI processed **7,000 tax returns**; accountants saved **31% of their time** on average; one job went from **180 hours/year to 15**. Tax agents run on **OpenAI's Codex**. |
| **Long Lake** (property management) | 18 businesses acquired, **$100M EBITDA in under two years**, margins doubled |
| **Crescendo** (contact centres) | **90% of frontline tickets resolved by AI**, 4× the margins of traditional operators |
| **Titan** (MSP / IT services) | 30%+ of workflows automated, targeting tripled net margins |
| **Dwelly** (UK real estate) | Problem resolution **50 days → 20**, margins doubled |
| **Thrive Holdings** | ~50 local accounting practices in two years; **$1B committed** to buy more |
| **General Catalyst** | **$1.5B allocated**, **$750M+ into 10+ companies** |

**Isenberg flags the caveat himself, twice.** On General Catalyst: *"they're self-reported, and none of these companies has been through a recession yet."* And as failure mode #6: *"Believing the headline numbers. Most published results in this space are self-reported by young companies that are raising money… Underwrite your deal on your own numbers."*

## Key Claims / Takeaways

### The three files

> *"3 little files do most of the work: **target-criteria.md** decides what you buy, **rules/** decides whether the agents can be trusted, and **corrections-log.md** is how the rules get better every week. If you set up nothing else, set up those."*

### The rulebook, now with a mechanism

The 09-20 guide introduced the rulebook as *"the written list of what 'correct' means in your niche."* This adds **how it gets built** — two prompts, run on a weekly cadence:

1. **Extraction by interview:** interview senior staff on *"the most common mistakes junior staff make, the clients who need special handling and why, the checks they always do before anything leaves the firm, and the situations where they'd stop and ask someone."* Turn every answer into a numbered rule with an example. **Mark any rule that conflicts with another. Nothing becomes active until they approve it.**
2. **Extraction by diff:** *"Compare each agent draft with the version a person approved. Classify every change as a factual error, a client preference, missing information, or a style change. Propose a rule for any correction that happened more than once. **Add each approved rule as a test case using the original input and the accepted output.**"*

> *"Anyone can use the same models. Nobody else has your list of every way they go wrong in this niche."*

### Separation of duties

> *"The reviewer can block but never ship, and the preparer can ship nothing on its own."*

Named as *"the most important design decision on this list."*

### A criterion for removing a human gate

Failure mode #5: *"An agent that can send client emails without a checkpoint will eventually send the wrong one to the wrong person. **Keep the checkpoints until the correction rate earns their removal.**"*

### The automation map

Diligence asks for **20–50 real anonymized completed jobs**. For each task: trigger, inputs, steps, output, human time, monthly frequency, **whether the output can be checked against a clear standard**, and what breaks if done wrong. Then classify: **automate now / automate with human review / assist only / keep human**. *"Multiply volume by hours, and you know where the margin is before you've signed anything. The automation map is the most valuable document in the deal."*

### Deal structure

**General Catalyst's, which he says is worth copying at any size:** ~**60–70% cash at close**, ~**30% rolled into equity for the founder**, *"so the person who built the relationships has a reason to stay."* At small scale: **SBA loan + seller note**, where *"the seller note does the same job as GC's rollover equity."*

### The first 100 days

- **1–30: change nothing the clients can see.** Meet every employee; announce with the former owner present; **run the agents in shadow mode** — they do the work in parallel, a person does it normally, you compare. Start rulebook interviews.
- **31–60: move the back office.** Highest-volume, lowest-risk task first, every draft reviewed. **Track minutes of human attention per job, weekly.** *"The people who used to prepare now review."*
- **61–100: expand carefully.** Two more tasks; client-comms agent on routine status only; monthly corrections-log review.

At day 100 you should know *"how much of the work the agents can do reliably, what it actually costs you in human review, and whether anyone important is unhappy."*

### The dashboard warning

Five numbers weekly per firm. *"If margin goes up while client retention goes down, you're selling the asset to pay for the renovation."*

### Why he thinks this is not just for funds

Five arguments: funds need big deals (*"a billion dollar vehicle can't spend its time on a bookkeeping firm doing $2 million a year"*); the tools are identical and subscription-priced; **you can be the integration** (*"the bottleneck that slows the funds down is the thing a solo founder does naturally"*); owners often prefer selling to a person; and SBA loans plus seller notes exist at this size.

### Target industries

Accounting/bookkeeping, property management, insurance agencies, IT managed services, medical billing, payroll, HOA management, title and escrow, freight brokerage, staffing. Filter: *"repeatable work, checkable output, recurring clients, fragmented ownership and a lot of owners near retirement."*

### What breaks

1. Buying faster than you can integrate. 2. Key people leave (*"I've seen this a lot, it's brutal"*). 3. Clients leave with the owner. 4. **Automating trust away** — *"the margin gains come from the back office. The value comes from the relationship."* 5. Agents with too much authority. 6. Believing the headline numbers.

## Judgment

**The transferable parts are the automation map, the two rulebook prompts, the preparer/reviewer split, and the correction-rate criterion for removing a gate.** Those are mechanisms, not framing, and three of the four are new to the wiki.

**The margin claim is the load-bearing assumption and it is unproven.** 5–10% → 30–40% is doing all the work in the arithmetic, and every figure supporting it is self-reported by a firm that is raising money. **Isenberg says this himself**, which is unusual and worth crediting — but the guide is still built on it.

**The illustrative math is round-numbered to the point of being a diagram**: buy at ~$800K, move margin 10% → 30%, *"the business is worth about three times what you paid at the same multiple."* He labels it illustrative and says *"the whole game is whether you can hold the margin."* Treat as shape, not model.

**Conflicts, stated:** links `ideabrowser.com` (his idea service), `LCA` (his design agency, *"we are the leading product design firm for AI"*), and his podcast. Nothing is disguised, but the guide is lead generation as well as content.
